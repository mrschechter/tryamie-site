How Naltrexone Helps Reduce Alcohol Cravings

Understanding this evidence-based approach to drinking less

If you've been wanting to cut back on drinking but find it harder than expected, you're not alone. Alcohol creates powerful neural pathways that make moderation genuinely difficult, even when you're motivated to change.

Naltrexone is an FDA-approved medication that can help break this cycle. It's not about willpower, it's about changing how your brain responds to alcohol so you can regain control.

78% reduction in heavy drinking days
50+ years of clinical research
FDA approved since 1994

What is Naltrexone?

Naltrexone is a medication that blocks opioid receptors in the brain. While it was originally developed for opioid addiction, researchers discovered it also helps reduce alcohol cravings and consumption.

Here's why: When you drink alcohol, your brain releases endorphins, creating feelings of pleasure and reward. Over time, your brain comes to expect and crave this endorphin rush, which is part of what makes alcohol habit-forming.

Naltrexone blocks the receptors that receive these endorphins. You can still drink, but the experience becomes less rewarding. Over time, your brain learns that alcohol isn't as pleasurable as it once was, and cravings naturally diminish.

How Naltrexone Reduces Cravings

The Science of Pharmacological Extinction

1

Take naltrexone 1 hour before drinking

2

Receptors Blocked

Opioid receptors are temporarily blocked

3

Drink Normally

Alcohol feels less rewarding than before

4

Brain Relearns

Over time, cravings naturally decrease

This process is called "pharmacological extinction." It's the same mechanism used to treat phobias and other learned behaviors. By removing the reward, the behavior gradually fades.

Key Insight

Naltrexone doesn't make you feel sick if you drink (that's a different medication called Antabuse). Instead, it makes drinking less enjoyable, allowing your brain to naturally lose interest over time.

The Sinclair Method

The Sinclair Method (TSM) is a specific approach to using naltrexone that has shown remarkable success rates. Named after Dr. John David Sinclair, who developed it, this method involves:

  1. Targeted use: Take naltrexone 1 hour before drinking, every time you drink
  2. Don't take it on days you don't drink: This preserves your natural endorphin system
  3. Be patient: The process typically takes 3-4 months to reach full effect
  4. Continue as needed: Most people use it long-term for maintenance

Clinical studies show the Sinclair Method achieves a 78% success rate, defined as either abstinence or controlled, moderate drinking.

Many people start noticing they're drinking less without trying as hard. The "voice" urging another drink becomes quieter.

Reduced interest in alcohol becomes more noticeable. You may find yourself stopping after one or two drinks naturally.

Most people reach their drinking goals. Some choose occasional social drinking; others find they prefer not drinking at all.

Who Can Benefit from Naltrexone?

Naltrexone may be a good fit if you:

  • Want to cut back but struggle with cravings
  • Find yourself drinking more than intended
  • Have tried willpower alone without lasting success
  • Are looking for a medical approach to moderation
  • Prefer to reduce drinking rather than quit entirely
  • Want to regain control over your relationship with alcohol

Naltrexone is not appropriate for everyone. You should not use it if you:

  • Currently use opioid medications (pain pills, etc.)
  • Are dependent on opioids
  • Have acute hepatitis or liver failure

Side Effects and Considerations

Most people tolerate naltrexone well. Common side effects, especially in the first week, may include:

  • Nausea: Usually mild and temporary; taking with food helps
  • Headache: Typically resolves within a few days
  • Dizziness: Usually mild
  • Fatigue: Some people feel tired initially

These side effects typically diminish within the first week or two. Serious side effects are rare but should be reported to your provider.

Important Note

Because naltrexone blocks opioid receptors, you cannot take opioid pain medications while using it. If you need pain relief, your provider can recommend alternatives. Always inform any healthcare provider that you're taking naltrexone.

Naltrexone vs. Other Approaches

Willpower Alone

Research shows that willpower-based approaches have about a 10% long-term success rate. This isn't because people lack discipline, it's because alcohol physically changes brain chemistry. Naltrexone addresses this biological component.

Traditional Abstinence Programs

Programs like AA work well for some people, particularly those seeking complete abstinence. Naltrexone can complement these programs or offer an alternative for those who prefer a moderation approach.

Therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy and other counseling approaches address the psychological aspects of drinking. Many people find the best results combining naltrexone with therapy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will I still feel drunk if I drink on naltrexone?
Yes, alcohol will still affect you normally in terms of intoxication and impairment. Naltrexone only blocks the pleasure/reward response, not the physical effects of alcohol.
Do I have to take naltrexone every day?
With the Sinclair Method, you only take it before drinking. If you take it daily, you take it every day regardless of drinking. Your provider will recommend the best approach for you.
Can I drink at all on naltrexone?
Yes. In fact, with the Sinclair Method, you should drink normally while on the medication. The goal is to retrain your brain by having less rewarding drinking experiences.
How long do I need to take naltrexone?
Most people see significant results within 3-4 months. Some continue taking it long-term for maintenance; others eventually stop and maintain their progress. This varies by individual.
Is naltrexone addictive?
No. Naltrexone has no abuse potential and is not addictive. You can stop taking it at any time without withdrawal symptoms.
Can I use naltrexone if I'm not an "alcoholic"?
Absolutely. Naltrexone is helpful for anyone who wants to reduce their drinking, regardless of whether they meet criteria for alcohol use disorder. Many people use it simply to regain control.
